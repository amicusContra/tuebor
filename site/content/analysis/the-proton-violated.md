## The Proton, Violated

Andy Yen named his company after a subatomic particle.

The proton. Positive charge. The thing that holds atoms together. The thing that makes matter possible. He chose the name because he wanted to build something fundamental — something that holds the digital world together the way protons hold the physical one.

He has a PhD in particle physics from Harvard. He did research at CERN — the place where they smash protons together to understand the universe. He spoke at TED about privacy. He spoke at the United Nations about online freedom. He built ProtonMail so that activists and journalists and whistleblowers could communicate without surveillance.

That's what he said.

Here's what he did.

## Incident 1: The French Activist (September 2021)

A climate activist in Paris used ProtonMail to organize. The account: `jmm18@protonmail.com`. French police wanted to know who was behind it.

Proton is Swiss. Not subject to French law. Not subject to EU requests. That was the whole point.

But French police routed their request through Europol to Swiss authorities. Swiss courts approved the order. And Andy Yen's company — the company built to protect exactly this person — **began logging the activist's IP address and handed it to police.**

The activist was identified. The activist was arrested.

Andy Yen's response: *"There was no possibility to appeal this particular request."*

Then Proton quietly removed the line *"we do not log IPs"* from their website.

They didn't tell users. They just... edited the page.

## Incident 2: The Phrack Journalists (August 2025)

Phrack is one of the oldest hacker publications in existence. Their journalists discovered evidence of North Korean cyber-espionage targeting South Korean government systems. They did what responsible security researchers do: they used ProtonMail to notify the affected agencies — KISA, KrCERT, the Ministry of Unification.

Responsible disclosure. Using encrypted email. Exactly what Proton was built for.

On August 15, 2025, Proton suspended the disclosure account. The next day, they suspended the journalist's personal account too.

The message: **"This account has been disabled due to a potential policy violation."**

Sound familiar?

The journalists appealed. Proton's response: *"Your account will cause further damage to our service, so we'll keep it suspended."*

They contacted Proton's legal department. **Eight times.** Silence.

They waited three weeks. Nothing.

So they went public. The story went viral on social media. Suddenly Proton cared. Andy Yen personally decided to reinstate two accounts — but only after the world was watching.

Proton's official statement: *"We stand with journalists."*

Their actual behavior: suspend first, ghost for three weeks, reinstate only under public pressure.

**Sources:** [The Intercept](https://theintercept.com/2025/09/12/proton-mail-journalist-accounts-suspended/), [CyberInsider](https://cyberinsider.com/proton-suspended-journalist-accounts-citing-term-of-service-violations/)

## Incident 3: ecoPrimal (October 2026)

Not spam. Not hacking. Not phishing. Not bulk mail.

Public records. To journalists. And lawyers.

The message: **"This account is not allowed to send messages due to a potential policy violation."**

Same message as Phrack. Same pattern. Same result.

Someone complained. Proton's automated system flagged the account. No human reviewed it. No appeal offered. No explanation given.

The privacy company silenced an investigative journalist sending public records to the Associated Press.
