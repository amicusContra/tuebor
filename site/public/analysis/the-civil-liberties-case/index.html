The Issues

1. Unauthorized Mass Data Collection

AI companies are scraping the open web at industrial scale for commercial training data. The legal framework is unsettled. This case has clean facts: one server, identified crawlers, documented violations, clear damages.

2. Residential Proxy Exploitation

Fleet crawlers are routing through residential proxy networks – using real people’s devices and internet connections as exit nodes. The proxy SDKs are embedded in free apps with inadequate disclosure. This is mass nonconsensual use of private computing resources for corporate AI training. It touches:

  • Fourth Amendment principles (warrantless use of private property)
  • Consumer protection (deceptive app practices)
  • Digital autonomy (who controls YOUR device?)

3. The AGPL Question

If AI models are trained on AGPL-licensed code, are the models derivative works? This is the open-source case of the decade. The answer determines whether open-source licenses protect code from AI training or whether the largest companies can take freely while giving nothing back.

4. The Power Asymmetry

One person with a $6 server vs. companies worth hundreds of billions. The individual has complete documentation and clear legal claims but no resources to litigate. The companies have limitless legal budgets and every incentive to bury this. This is WHY public interest law exists.

5. The Right to Monitor Your Own Infrastructure

The server owner has the right to log, analyze, and publish findings about who accesses their infrastructure. This is not surveillance – it is property defense. Establishing this principle clearly matters for every individual who runs a server, every small business with a website, every independent creator whose work is being consumed at scale.

6. First Amendment and Public Record

Everything in this case is published as a public record. The analysis. The evidence. The legal claims. This article. The entire body of work is protected speech, protected journalism, protected research. Any attempt to suppress it raises First Amendment concerns.
