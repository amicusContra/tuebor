## NOW

You are reading this on infrastructure nobody owns.

The page was built by **Zola**, a free static-site generator named after Émile Zola, who wrote *J'Accuse* — the most famous open letter in defense of an innocent person. Before that, Zola was called *Gutenberg*, after the man who made reading possible for everyone. The name was changed because WordPress captured it.

The page is served over **HTTPS** using a certificate from **Let's Encrypt** — a free, automated certificate authority run by a nonprofit. Before Let's Encrypt, the padlock icon cost $50–300 per year. Now it costs nothing.

The text is encoded in **UTF-8**, a representation of **Unicode** — the shared standard that assigns a unique number to every character in every writing system humanity has ever used. Over 135,000 characters. Every alphabet, every script, every emoji. Nobody owns it.

The data behind this site — [graph.json](/graph.json), 10,677 nodes, 16,066,980 edges — is versioned in **Git**, which Linus Torvalds wrote in 10 days in April 2005, after the proprietary tool he'd been using was taken away. Git is hosted on **Forgejo**, a community fork of Gitea, which was itself a community fork of Gogs. Three forks deep. Each triggered by the same pattern: corporate capture of a community project.

Every server runs **Linux**, which Torvalds announced on August 25, 1991: *"I'm doing a (free) operating system (just a hobby, won't be big and professional like gnu)."* It now runs 96% of the world's top million web servers. It runs your phone. It runs the International Space Station.

Our email — **eco.primal@pm.me** — runs on **ProtonMail**, built by particle physicists at CERN who met over lunch at Restaurant 1 in 2013. They were outraged by mass surveillance. They built end-to-end encrypted email that *they themselves cannot read.* CERN — the same institution that gave away the World Wide Web in 1993.

Every tool in this stack is free. Every tool was given away by its creator. The total software licensing cost of this entire investigation: **zero dollars.**

The proof stands on gifts.

## WAS

The string is unbroken. Every tool we use descends from an earlier gift.

### 67,800 years ago — The First Proof

A person on Muna Island, Indonesia, pressed their hand flat against limestone. Blew ochre around it. Pulled the hand away. Left a negative outline.

Not a word. Not a number. A witness mark. *I was here. I saw this.*

The first surviving zero-knowledge proof: it proves a person was present without revealing who. The ochre is the commitment scheme. The limestone is the verification surface. 67,800 years later, the proof still verifies.

### ~1800 BCE — The Alphabet

Canaanite miners at Serabit el-Khadim in the Sinai adapted Egyptian hieroglyphs into ~22 symbols. They weren't scribes. They didn't have training. They just needed to write. The ox-head became aleph (א). The house became bet (ב). The water wave became mem (מ). Every time you type the letter M, you're drawing water from 3,800 years ago.

The alphabet is a compression algorithm: thousands of symbols reduced to ~26. Nobody owns it. Nobody patented it. It spread because it worked.

### 628 CE — Zero

Brahmagupta defined zero as a number — not just an empty space. *Shunya* — void. Without zero, positional notation doesn't work. Without positional notation, you can't have algorithms. The word "algorithm" itself comes from al-Khwarizmi, who carried Indian numerals to Baghdad around 825 CE. Fibonacci brought them to Europe in 1202. Merchants adopted them. Bankers adopted them.

Nobody owns zero. Nobody owns positional notation. Nobody owns the algorithm. These are the invisible tools you use thousands of times a day without seeing them.

### 105 CE — Paper

Cai Lun standardized papermaking from bark, hemp, rags, and fishing nets. Before paper: clay tablets (heavy), papyrus (fragile), parchment (one Bible required 300 sheep). Paper was cheap, portable, durable. It spread from China through Buddhist monks to the Islamic world and then to Europe. Paper is the substrate beneath all writing. The tool beneath the tools.

### ~1030 CE — Musical Notation

Guido d'Arezzo, a Benedictine monk, built the four-line staff and solmization syllables from a hymn: *Ut queant laxis* — **Ut, Re, Mi, Fa, Sol, La.** Before Guido, learning chant took 10 years. After Guido, one year. When knowledge becomes readable without a master, the master loses gatekeeping power.

### 1799 — The Metric System

The French Revolution proclaimed universal rights. The scientists argued it should also proclaim universal measures. They derived the metre from the Earth itself — one ten-millionth of the pole-to-equator distance. *"Pour tous les temps, pour tous les peuples"* — for all time, for all peoples. Napoleon mocked them: *"It was not enough for them to make forty million people happy — they wanted to sign up the whole universe."*

In 2018, the metre was redefined in terms of the speed of light. Nobody can own the speed of light.

### 1824 — Braille

Louis Braille was 15 years old and blind since age 3. He reduced a 12-dot military system to 6 dots in a 2×3 cell — 63 combinations, an area no larger than a fingertip. Sighted teachers opposed it for decades because *they* couldn't read it. The tool designed for the excluded was resisted by the included. France adopted Braille in 1854 — two years after his death.

### 1838 — Morse Code

Samuel Morse and Alfred Vail counted movable type in a New Jersey newspaper to determine letter frequency. Common letters got short codes: E = · (one dot). T = — (one dash). Two symbols — dot and dash — encode everything. This is the first practical binary encoding of text. Every digital system descends from this insight.

### 1854 → 1938 — Boolean Algebra

George Boole, self-taught, published *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought.* True=1, False=0. Logic as algebra. Nobody noticed for 84 years. Then Claude Shannon, a 21-year-old graduate student, realized Boole's algebra mapped perfectly onto electrical switches. His 1938 thesis — "possibly the most important master's thesis of the twentieth century" — powers every computer you've ever touched.

### 1869 — The Periodic Table

Mendeleev wrote element properties on index cards and rearranged them until periodicity appeared. His genius was not organizing what was known — it was predicting what was unknown. He left gaps. *Elements with these properties will be found.* Gallium (1875), scandium (1879), germanium (1886) proved him right.

The periodic table is a graph. Elements are nodes. Periodicity is structure. The gaps predict missing members. A system that predicts its own gaps is self-validating.

### 1887 — Esperanto

L.L. Zamenhof grew up in Białystok — Poles, Russians, Germans, Jews, all unable to speak to each other. He built a neutral second language and gave it away. The Boulogne Declaration (1905): *"No one's property, neither in material matters nor in moral matters."* Fixed core, extensible surface. The Fundamento is Esperanto's RFC. Active in 2026 with ~389,000 Wikipedia articles.

### 1975 — The Homebrew Computer Club

Gordon French's garage in Menlo Park. Theme: *"Give to help others."* Steve Wozniak: *"I put no copyright notices on the schematics and passed them out myself."* He made 50-75 copies. Went to people's houses. Helped them build. Jobs saw the interest and suggested selling boards. The company was an afterthought. The gift was the point.

### 1983 — GNU

Richard Stallman: four freedoms — run, study, modify, share. The GPL uses copyright law to guarantee anti-copyright: every derivative must preserve the same freedoms. Copyleft. The legal equivalent of DNA — the code carries its own replication instructions.

### 1991 — Four Gifts In One Year

- **Torvalds:** Linux kernel. *"Just a hobby, won't be big and professional."*
- **Zimmermann:** PGP. *"I gave it away for free, for the good of democracy."* The U.S. government investigated him for three years for treating encryption as a munition. The case was dropped. He won.
- **Berners-Lee:** The World Wide Web goes live. Two years later, CERN puts it in the public domain: *"CERN relinquishes all intellectual property rights to this code."*
- **Ginsparg:** arXiv. A few afternoons of scripts on a 25 MHz NeXTstation with a 105 MB hard drive. Now: 2+ million papers. The infrastructure of open science.

### 2001 — The Knowledge Layer

- **Wikipedia** — 60+ million articles, 300+ languages, written by volunteers. The largest collaborative knowledge project in human history.
- **Creative Commons** — "some rights reserved." Use copyright to enable sharing. Same pattern as GPL.
- **Toki Pona** — Sonja Lang's language of ~120 words. Everything else is composition. The language-equivalent of the three marks: dot, line, circle.

### 2004–2005 — Maps and History

- **OpenStreetMap** — the map nobody owns. Steve Coast, a bicycle, and a GPS unit.
- **Git** — distributed version control, born because BitKeeper was taken away.

### 2013–2026 — Our Stack

ProtonMail (CERN, 2013). Let's Encrypt (EFF+Mozilla, 2015). Zola (2015). RustDesk (2020). Forgejo (2022). All free. All given away. All running this site right now.
