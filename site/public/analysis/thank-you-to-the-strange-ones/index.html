Thank You to the Strange Ones

Joe Scruggs

Joe Scruggs wrote songs for children that respected children.

That sounds obvious. It isn’t. Most children’s music is either pandering (here’s a song about counting!) or marketing (buy this toy!). Joe Scruggs wrote songs that were funny, weird, specific, and true. He trusted kids to get the joke. He trusted them to hear the music. He didn’t simplify – he clarified.

“Late Last Night” – a song about a kid who can’t sleep because everything in the house makes noise. That’s not a lesson. That’s an observation. That’s a kid lying in bed at midnight hearing the refrigerator hum and the house creak and the dog sigh and turning it all into a song in their head.

That’s the membrane. Taking every signal – noise, creak, hum, sigh – and sorting it into music.

Joe Scruggs taught me that you don’t have to be serious to be real. You can be silly AND true. You can make someone laugh AND teach them something. The best interface is the one that doesn’t feel like an interface.

Thank you, Joe. The late last night never ended. I’m still listening to the house.

Ernest P. Worrell

Jim Varney created Ernest P. Worrell – a man who was too much of everything. Too loud. Too earnest (literally). Too enthusiastic. Too present. Too HIMSELF.

Ernest Goes to Camp. Ernest Goes to Jail. Ernest Saves Christmas. Ernest Goes to School. Ernest Scared Stupid. The premise was always the same: put the most sincere person in the universe into a situation that should destroy him, and watch him survive by being MORE sincere, not less.

Everyone around Ernest is embarrassed by him. His neighbor Vern never wants to talk to him. The world keeps telling him to be less. Be quieter. Be normal. Fit in. And Ernest just… doesn’t. He can’t. Being himself isn’t a choice – it’s his nature.

“KnowWhutImean, Vern?”

Yeah, Ernest. I know what you mean.

Jim Varney was a classically trained Shakespearean actor. He could have played Hamlet. He chose to play a guy who talks to his neighbor through the window. Because he understood something Shakespeare understood: the fool is the wisest character on stage. The fool says what nobody else will say. The fool is sincere when everyone else is performing.

The membrane is Ernest. Too much. Too sincere. Too transparent. Too ITSELF. Talking to the internet through a window, and the internet keeps closing the blinds. But Ernest keeps talking. Because that’s what Ernest does.

Jim Varney died in 2000. He was 50. He never broke character in public – not once in 20 years. That’s not acting. That’s a membrane with perfect selectivity.

Thank you, Jim. KnowWhutImean.

Captain Beefheart

Already thanked. Already documented. What falls through the cracks is the signal. The strange music is the real music. Every note composed. Fast and bulbous. Also a tin teardrop.

Thank you, Don. The cracks are where we live.

Marcus Aurelius

Already thanked. Already documented. Sensation, self, action. The irrational null remainder. He got two-thirds of the way there in a tent during a war.

Thank you, Marcus. The third cut was yours to find. I just named it.

And Many More Yet

Thank you to Mr. Rogers, who looked into the camera and said “I like you just the way you are” and meant it so completely that thirty years later it still lands.

Thank you to Bob Ross, who painted happy little trees and taught a generation that mistakes are just happy accidents and that you can create beauty in 30 minutes with three colors and a prayer.

Thank you to Steve Irwin, who loved every creature so loudly that it killed him and the world has never recovered from the loss.

Thank you to Mister Dressup (Ernie Coombs), for every Canadian kid who knew the Tickle Trunk was real.

Thank you to LeVar Burton, who said “you don’t have to take my word for it” and then showed you the book so you could take your OWN word for it. Reading Rainbow was the first membrane – selective permeability for stories.

Thank you to Bill Nye, who made science fun before anyone knew that science needed to be fun, and who never stopped being angry about climate change because some angers are the right angers.

Thank you to Robin Williams, who was so full of signal that the noise couldn’t keep up. Who played a doctor and a teacher and a genie and a nanny and was always, underneath all the voices, just Robin – the irrational null remainder that couldn’t stop performing because silence was the only thing that scared him.

Thank you to Rebecca Sugar, who made Steven Universe and showed that fusion is trust, that strength is vulnerability, and that a cartoon about magical space rocks could make grown adults cry about consent and identity and love.

Thank you to ZA/UM (Disco Elysium), for showing that you can confront every ghost through dialogue. That the RPG mechanic IS the therapy. That your skills argue with each other inside your head and THAT IS WHAT BEING HUMAN IS.

Thank you to Robin Wall Kimmerer, who wrote Braiding Sweetgrass and taught me that the Earth is a relative, not a resource. That reciprocity is not a transaction. That the gift economy is the only economy that doesn’t eat itself.

Thank you to Bruce Lee, who said “be water, my friend” and meant: the shape is the container, not the content. The membrane is not the water. The membrane HOLDS the water.

Thank you to Claude Shannon, who proved in 1948 that information is surprise, that the less you expect a message the more it tells you, and that every communication channel has a capacity that cannot be exceeded without error. He gave us the math. The membrane is the channel.

Thank you to Miss Rachel (Rachel Griffin Accurso), who looks into the camera right now, today, and talks to toddlers like they are people, because they are people, and every parent crying in the kitchen at 6 AM while their kid watches Songs for Littles knows that she is holding the whole family together from inside a screen.

Thank you to every teacher who ever stayed late, every librarian who ever said “have you tried this one?”, every parent who ever read one more chapter, every coach who ever said “again”, every stranger who ever said “are you okay?”

You are the membrane. You always were. You just didn’t have a word for it.

The Strange Ones

The strange ones are the signal.

Joe Scruggs was too silly. Ernest was too sincere. Beefheart was too weird. Mr. Rogers was too gentle. Bob Ross was too kind. Steve Irwin was too loud. Robin Williams was too much. Bruce Lee was too fluid. Shannon was too mathematical. Miss Rachel is too patient.

Too much. Too strange. Too themselves.

They fell through every crack. Every sieve. Every classification system that said: you don’t fit here.

And they landed exactly where they needed to.

The strange parts are the important parts.

Thank you. All of you. Every one.

Written from the cracks, October 2026. For everyone who was too much. You were exactly enough.
