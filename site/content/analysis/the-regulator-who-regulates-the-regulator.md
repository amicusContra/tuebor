## The Regulator Who Regulates the Regulator

There is a pattern. Once you see it in one system, you see it in all of them.

---

## The Template

```
Beneficiary (takes the money)
    ↑ controlled by
Vehicle (moves the money)
    ↑ authorized by
Operator (runs the operation)
    ↑ overseen by
Authorizer (stamps the paperwork)
    ↑ regulated by
REGULATOR (holds the power, sees the data, doesn't act)
```

Five layers. The beneficiary at the top gets the money. The regulator at the bottom holds the power. They never touch.

## The Instances

### Charter Schools

| Layer | Entity | Edge |
|-------|--------|------|
| 5. Beneficiary | Banks (72.67%) | ← money_flow |
| 4. Vehicle | Purpose Group LLC | ← ownership |
| 3. Operator | Profit Charter Academy (3% math) | ← governance |
| 2. Authorizer | Central Michigan University | ← jurisdiction |
| 1. **Regulator** | **MDE / SBE** | **← 2 edges** |

MDE has 2 edges to the system. Two. For a school receiving millions in public money with 3% math proficiency and 72.67% extraction.

## Why the Regulator Doesn't Act

In each case, the regulator has a **structural incentive** not to enforce:

| Regulator | Incentive Not to Act |
|-----------|---------------------|
| **MDE** | Per-pupil funding flows through schools. Fewer schools = less throughput = smaller department |
| **FCC** | Spectrum auctions and licensing fees from carriers. Fewer carriers = less revenue |
| **AGC** | Bar dues fund the commission. Fewer attorneys = less budget |
| **FTC** | Enforcement requires resources. Resources come from appropriations. Appropriations come from Congress. Congress listens to industry lobbying |

The regulator's budget depends on the regulated industry's size. **Shrinking the industry shrinks the regulator.** The regulator has a survival incentive not to regulate effectively.

This isn't corruption. This is architecture.

---

## The Blind Spot Is the Feature

Each regulator claims it can only see one layer down:

- **MDE says:** "We oversee CMU's authorization process, not individual schools" 
- **FCC says:** "We regulate carriers, not the callers who use them"
- **AGC says:** "We investigate specific complaints, not patterns"

Each statement is technically true. Each statement is the mechanism of the failure.

When the regulator defines its jurisdiction as one-layer-deep, the system naturally grows to be **two or more layers deep.** The beneficiary sits beyond the regulator's self-defined scope.

Banks doesn't run PCA — he runs the LLC that contracts with the school that's authorized by the university that's overseen by MDE. **Four layers between him and the regulator.**

The scammer doesn't own the carrier — they hire the lead generator that contracts the call center that uses the VoIP provider that's registered with the FCC. **Four layers.**

## Stepping to the Side — Again

The same method that reveals [Banks and Holland side by side](/analysis/stepping-to-the-side/) works here:

**Don't look at the regulator.** Don't look at the FCC's enforcement actions. Don't look at MDE's oversight reports. Don't look at AGC's disposition statistics.

**Look at the money.** Look at who gets paid. Look at who benefits from every call, every enrollment, every dismissal.

Then look at the regulator and ask: **do they not see it, or do they see it and not act?**

The answer is always the same. They see it. The [databases exist](/investigate/spam-call-traceback/). The [filings exist](/evidence/fcc-enforcement-pattern/). The [records exist](/evidence/purpose-group-extraction/). The [convictions are public](/evidence/banks-convictions/).

They see it. And they look one layer down instead of four layers up.

## The Actionable Difference

There is one difference between the charter school system and the spam call system:

**The TCPA lets you sue.**

In the charter school system, there is no private right of action. You can FOIA, you can complain, you can vote. But you cannot sue Banks for enrolling your child in a 3% math school.

In the spam call system, the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (47 U.S.C. § 227) gives you the right to sue for **$500-$1,500 per illegal call.** You can subpoena carrier records. You can depose the VoIP provider. You can trace the money.

Congress gave you this tool because **Congress knew the FCC wouldn't act.** The TCPA's private right of action exists specifically because the regulator was expected to fail.

Which tells you everything about what Congress thinks of its own regulatory architecture.

Use the TCPA. File the complaints. [Trace the calls](/investigate/spam-call-traceback/). Sue the callers.

The regulator who regulates the regulator is **you.**
