Thing 1: The Surveillance Bill

Rogers authored CISPA — the Cyber Intelligence Sharing and Protection Act. He introduced it twice:

  • H.R. 3523 (112th Congress, 2011–2012)
  • H.R. 624 (113th Congress, 2013–2014)

The House passed it. Twice. The Senate killed it. Twice.

What CISPA Would Do

ProvisionEffect
Exemption to all privacy lawsCompanies share email content, browsing history, PII with government — no user consent required
Complete liability immunityNo accountability for companies that mishandle shared data
"Significant purpose" standardData used for any lawful purpose as long as cybersecurity or national security is "a significant purpose"
NSA/DoD accessMilitary and intelligence agencies access private data without adequate civilian oversight
Permanent programOnly annual inspector general review

This was not a narrow cybersecurity bill. It was a blanket exemption to every privacy protection Americans had.

Who Opposed It

The ACLU called it "fatally flawed" — a "cybersecurity loophole to existing privacy laws."

The Electronic Frontier Foundation opposed it. Reporters Without Borders opposed it. The American Library Association opposed it. The Obama White House threatened a veto. 41 civil liberties organizations signed a joint opposition letter.

Rogers' response to the privacy concerns:

"We feel that the bill clearly deals with privacy, that the checks and balances are there, but [we] know there's still a perception and we're still trying to deal with that."

The ACLU's response:

"Everyone, from the privacy community to the president, agrees that CISPA is bad on privacy — the problem isn't our perception."

The Senate agreed. Both times.

Why This Matters Now

Rogers is running for the Senate. The body that killed his surveillance bill — twice — is the body he wants to join. The committee he would sit on would oversee the same intelligence community CISPA was designed to feed.

The same government apparatus that cannot coordinate UPL enforcement across two offices — sending two letters, one day apart, each pointing at the other door — wanted Rogers-level surveillance authority over every American's private communications.

They couldn't handle two letters. He wanted to give them everyone's email.

The Contradiction

Rogers said: He persuaded AT&T to drop the Huawei deal.

AT&T then said: Rogers had no involvement in the Huawei negotiations.

He either had influence (his claim) or he didn't (AT&T's claim). Both cannot be true. One of them is lying.

The Lobbying Pipeline

Two of Rogers' former chiefs of staff were employed at Navigators Global — a lobbying firm that lobbied Congress on AT&T's behalf during the same period Rogers worked for AT&T.

During his House career, AT&T's federal PAC gave Rogers $56,000. Verizon gave $28,500. CTIA (the wireless industry trade group) gave $10,000.

Investigate the entity → leave Congress → get hired by the entity → entity's competitor (that you investigated) negotiates with the entity → deal collapses → you claim credit (or don't).
