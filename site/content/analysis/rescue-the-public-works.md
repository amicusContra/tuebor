## The Graveyard of Public Works

Your taxes paid for this. All of it. And it's dying.

- **152 OER repositories** studied — a "large proportion" now inactive or defunct
- **19 of 59** NEH-funded digital humanities websites gone offline
- Average free-use educational website lives **5 years** before disappearing
- Average website lives **100 days** before the URL breaks
- OERF (Open Education Resource Foundation) — served **140 million visitors** from 120 countries — **shutting down in 2026**

The pattern: Government funds creation → content goes online → funding ends → server dies → content vanishes → taxpayers pay again.

The membrane breaks this pattern.

### National Educational Television (NET) — The Pre-PBS Era

**10,000+ programs** from 1952-1972. Public affairs, science, arts, education. Digitized by Indiana University. Many in the **public domain**. This is the foundation PBS was built on — and most people don't know it exists.

- [American Archive of Public Broadcasting](https://americanarchive.org/special_collections/net-catalog) — Catalog of 10,000+ programs
- [Indiana University NET Collection](https://collections.libraries.indiana.edu/IULMIA/exhibits?tags=National+Educational+Television) — Hundreds of digitized public domain programs

## The Problem

None of these systems talk to each other. A child in Detroit who needs to learn to read has to:

1. Know that Internet Archive exists
2. Know that Sesame Street videos are there
3. Navigate a website designed for archivists, not children
4. Hope the file isn't corrupted or taken down
5. Have a parent who knows how to find it

Meanwhile, Brian Banks' charter school gets **$4.9 million per year** in public funding and delivers **3% math proficiency**.

The content exists. The funding existed. The infrastructure was built. And then it was all left to rot on servers nobody maintains, behind URLs nobody remembers, in formats nobody can play.

## The Rescue Operation

### Search
Systematically catalog every piece of freely-licensed or public domain educational content across:
- Internet Archive (video, audio, text)
- Federal agencies (NASA, NOAA, USGS, Smithsonian, LOC)
- Dead/dying OER repositories (recover before they vanish)
- Department of Education openly-licensed grant deliverables
- NET collection (10,000+ pre-PBS programs)

### Scrub
- Verify license status (public domain, CC0, CC-BY, open license)
- Check file integrity (corrupt files, broken links, missing assets)
- Normalize formats (transcode to modern codecs, standard resolutions)
- Add metadata (subject, age range, learning objectives, accessibility)

### Fix
- Repair broken content (fill gaps, add captions, fix encoding)
- Organize by age, subject, and learning pathway
- Create curriculum maps linking content to learning standards
- Add gAIa adaptive layer — AI tutoring over the rescued content

### Bring Inside the Membrane
- Git-sign everything — content-addressed, BLAKE3 hashed, immutable
- Replicate across three surfaces — membrane, sovereign git, GitHub
- Auto-rebuild on push — if one surface dies, the others rebuild
- **The content can never be lost again**

## The Legal Foundation

| Law / Policy | What It Says |
|---|---|
| **17 U.S.C. § 105** | Works created by federal employees are not eligible for U.S. copyright |
| **Open Licensing Requirement (2 C.F.R. § 3474.20)** | Dept. of Education grant deliverables must be openly licensed since 2017 |
| **Foundations for Evidence-Based Policymaking Act (2018)** | Federal agencies must maintain public data under open license |
| **CC0 (Creative Commons Zero)** | USGS, Smithsonian use CC0 — no copyright, no restrictions |
| **Fair Use (17 U.S.C. § 107)** | Educational use, criticism, commentary are protected |

The law is already on our side. The content is already free. It's just not **organized, preserved, or accessible** to the children who need it.
