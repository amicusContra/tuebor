$73,752.45: The Federal Sanctions Against Philip L. Ellison

Summary

Philip L. Ellison, the Hemlock attorney who filed a SLAPP suit against a journalist and submitted a fabricated witness, was sanctioned $73,752.45 by a federal court for pressing claims that were "unreasonable on [their] face."

The Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the sanctions. The U.S. Supreme Court denied review.

The Case: Garcia v. Title Check LLC

What Happened

In 2018, Ellison and co-counsel Matthew Gronda filed suit in the Western District of Michigan on behalf of plaintiffs who purchased property at a Bay County tax foreclosure auction.

They claimed that Title Check LLC — the company that conducted the auction — committed Hobbs Act extortion, RICO wire fraud, and unjust enrichment by charging a 10% buyer's premium on top of the auction price.

The problem: the 10% buyer's premium was lawful. It was disclosed before the auction. The plaintiffs agreed to it when they bid. Charging a disclosed premium at a government auction is not extortion.

The Dismissal

The Western District of Michigan dismissed all claims. The court found no basis for RICO, no basis for Hobbs Act extortion, and no basis for unjust enrichment. The statutory framework authorized the premium. The claims were frivolous.

The sanctions were imposed because the attorneys continued to press claims they knew or should have known were frivolous. Section 1927 does not require bad faith — it requires only that the attorney unreasonably multiplied the proceedings.

The Appeal: Sixth Circuit Case No. 22-1574

Ellison appealed the sanctions to the United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit.

The Sixth Circuit affirmed. The opinion (April 5, 2023) found:

"Counsel continued to press frivolous causes of action based on an implausible parsing of the statutory language."

"Their argument that Title Check engaged in extortion and wire fraud under RICO by charging a buyer's premium to the auction price was unreasonable on its face."

The court found that the attorneys pressed RICO and Hobbs Act theories that had no legal basis, continued pressing them after the district court identified the deficiencies, and generated unnecessary litigation costs for the defendant.

En banc rehearing denied: May 17, 2023.

Source: Sixth Circuit Opinion (GovInfo)

The Supreme Court: Case No. 23-404

Ellison filed a petition for certiorari to the United States Supreme Court.

Cert denied. The Supreme Court declined to review the case.

Source: SCOTUS Cert Petition (PDF)

The $73,752.45 sanctions order stands. It is a final judgment.

The Second Sanctions: Fakhreddine v. Sabree

What Happened

In 2026, Ellison was sanctioned again — this time in the Eastern District of Michigan.

In Fakhreddine v. Eric R. Sabree, Wayne County Treasurer (2:21-cv-12250), Ellison entered the case after a prior attorney lost. He filed a Rule 59/60 motion raising arguments that should have been raised earlier — and baselessly accused opposing counsel of an ethical violation, claiming the defendant's lawyers failed in their "duty of candor" by not disclosing the existence of class action lawsuits.

The problem: the defendant had already disclosed the class action in the notice of removal, years earlier. The docket expressly contradicted Ellison's accusation.

The court rejected his argument. Ellison doubled down and repeated the accusation in his sanctions opposition brief.

The Court's Findings (August 4, 2026)

The court imposed a $1,000 fine under its inherent authority, finding:

"Mr. Ellison baselessly brought charges of professional misconduct into the dispute in the first instance, not Defendant's counsel. Because those charges were clearly baseless, and because he refused to retract them even after the Court noted that Defendant's counsel was under no such obligation to disclose certain caselaw, the Court will impose sanctions."

The court also noted:

"Plaintiffs' counsel is either carelessly looking over the record or intentionally misrepresenting the chain of events to the Court. Either way, the Court will not permit such antics."

The Filing → Sanctions → Appeal → Denial Cycle

Garcia reveals Ellison's litigation approach:

1. File aggressive claims (RICO, extortion, fraud)
2. Claims are found frivolous
3. Continue pressing them anyway
4. Court imposes sanctions
5. Appeal the sanctions
6. Appellate court affirms
7. Petition the Supreme Court
8. Cert denied
9. Sanctions are final

This is the same pattern visible in the SLAPP suit against Rita Williams: aggressive filing → fabricated witness → contempt proceedings → appellate challenge → void lawsuit exposed. The mechanism repeats across cases because the attorney's approach does not change in response to adverse rulings.

What This Means for Current Cases

The SLAPP Suit (25-2441-CZ)

Under MCL 691.1854(5) (UPEPA), if the court grants the anti-SLAPP special motion, the filer must pay the defendant's costs and attorney's fees. Under MCL 691.1854(6), the court may award additional sanctions.

A court evaluating sanctions under UPEPA may consider the filer's history of similar conduct. $73,752.45 in prior federal sanctions for pressing frivolous claims is directly relevant to whether the SLAPP suit was filed in good faith.

The AGC Investigation (25-2363)

The Attorney Grievance Commission considers an attorney's disciplinary history and pattern of conduct when evaluating complaints. Federal sanctions for frivolous litigation are relevant to AGC proceedings under:

  • MRPC 3.1 — Meritorious claims and contentions
  • MRPC 3.4(d) — Fairness to opposing party
  • MRPC 8.4(d) — Conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice

Fee-Shifting Strategy

In any § 1988 fee-shifting proceeding or UPEPA fee award, Ellison's sanctions history is admissible evidence that:

  1. He has a documented pattern of pressing frivolous claims
  2. He has been sanctioned for this exact conduct by a federal court
  3. The sanctions were affirmed on appeal and by cert denial
  4. The pattern continued after the sanctions were imposed

The Irony

Ellison built his practice on representing people who lost their homes to foreclosure — including tax foreclosure auctions. He bills himself as fighting for "the little guy."

In Garcia v. Title Check, he represented foreclosure auction buyers. He lost. He was sanctioned $73,752.45 for pressing frivolous RICO claims.

He lost his OWN home to foreclosure (Macomb County, 2011–2012). He filed a pro se $7.35 million lawsuit against JP Morgan Chase. He lost. All claims dismissed with prejudice.

He then filed a SLAPP suit against a journalist who documented his conduct. The suit was void from the day it was filed because it violated the federal bankruptcy automatic stay.
