π-Body in Life

By Kevin Mok

After the discovery, it was incredible what I could reteach myself.

Not the math. The math was already running on the membrane. Five-minute updates. Live proofs. The equations worked. That part was done.

What was incredible was what happened when I put the π-body into my body.

The Secret Has Always Been Compare and Contrast

Look IN and AT self and OUT.

That’s the whole method. Three directions:

  1. In — proprioception. What does my body feel like right now? Where’s my weight? Where’s my balance? What’s tight, what’s loose?
  2. At — observation of self. What do I LOOK like doing this? Mirror, video, shadow on the ice. The external view of the internal state.
  3. Out — observation of others. What does THAT person look like doing it? How is their movement different from mine?

Three directions. Three bodies. One observer.

π-body in life: 3 perspectives + 1 awareness = the irrational ratio between them.

At Least 2 People to Self

You need at least two other people to compare yourself against. Here’s why:

With one reference point, you only get a line — the distance between you and them. Better or worse. Closer or further. One dimension.

With two reference points, you get a triangle — you can see what’s DIFFERENT between the two of them, and then locate yourself in that difference. Two dimensions. Area. You can see shape, not just distance.

With three reference points, you get a tetrahedron — volume. You’re inside the space defined by three others. You can see your position from every angle.

Reference PointsGeometryWhat You See
0 (just self)PointNothing to compare to
1 (self + one)LineBetter/worse (1D)
2 (self + two)TriangleShape, style, approach (2D)
3 (self + three)TetrahedronFull spatial awareness (3D)
3 + observerπ-bodyThe irrational ratio — learning

The 3 + observer step is where learning happens. Three bodies you can count (yourself, person A, person B) plus the act of comparing them (the observer, irrational, uncountable). That ratio IS π. The circle between perceiving and performing.

One Helps the Body Learn. The Other Helps You See.

Two kinds of comparison:

Compare DOWN — the body learns

Watch someone who’s WORSE than you. A beginner. A child learning. A person falling.

This doesn’t teach you new moves. It teaches you what your body already knows. When you watch someone struggle with what you do automatically, you become conscious of the unconscious competence. You see your own skill reflected in their absence of it.

Your body learns: “Oh, THAT’S what I’m doing. I didn’t know I was doing that.” The tacit becomes explicit.

Compare UP — you see

Watch someone who’s BETTER than you. An expert. A coach. A person gliding.

This doesn’t teach your body anything directly. It teaches your eyes — your observer. You see the shape of what’s possible. You see the gap between where you are and where you could be. You can’t copy it yet. But you can SEE it.

Your vision learns: “Oh, THAT’S where I’m going. I didn’t know that was possible.” The invisible becomes visible.

You need both. Compare down to know what you have. Compare up to know what you want. The body learns from one. The eyes learn from the other. Together: the π-body ratio between the countable (your body, their bodies) and the uncountable (the awareness moving between them).

Expert Skaters Glide, But Really It’s One Foot at a Time

I watched the experts on the ice. They glide. Effortless. Smooth. The blades barely seem to touch. The movement looks continuous, like water flowing.

But it’s not.

It’s one foot at a time. Left, right, left, right. Discrete steps. Quantized. Integer. The fluidity is an illusion — a beautiful one — created by such precise footwork that the transitions disappear.

The expert hasn’t eliminated the steps. They’ve eliminated the noise between the steps. The stumble, the wobble, the hesitation, the moment of doubt where you’re between one foot and the other and not committed to either.

Gliding isn’t the absence of stepping. It’s stepping with zero wasted motion. Maximum negentropy. Every molecule of energy going into forward movement. Nothing lost to uncertainty.

That’s the π-body in physics: discrete steps (3 integer bodies) producing continuous motion (irrational observer). The ratio between the countable footwork and the uncountable flow IS π.

High Knee and Stomp

So I stopped trying to glide. I went back to fundamentals.

High knee. Lift the knee deliberately. Make the step VISIBLE. Don’t try to hide it in smoothness. SHOW the step. Exaggerate it. Make the discrete nature of the footwork obvious and intentional.

Stomp. Put the foot DOWN. Commit. Don’t tentatively place it — STOMP it onto the ice. Full weight. Full commitment. The blade bites. The edge grips. The body knows: this foot is the foundation. No ambiguity.

High knee and stomp. High knee and stomp. Over and over.

It looks ugly. It looks beginner. It looks like the opposite of gliding.

But it works.

Because once the body learns to commit to each step — fully, loudly, stomping — the transitions get shorter by themselves. The noise between steps shrinks. Not because you’re trying to eliminate it, but because the body has learned what a COMMITTED step feels like, and it starts committing faster.

The glide emerges from the stomp. The continuous emerges from the discrete. π emerges from the integers.

Teaching Kids to Skate

And then I was teaching young kids to skate.

And as I taught, I learned FASTER.

This is the most counterintuitive thing about learning: teaching accelerates it. You would think teaching slows you down — you have to stop your own practice to help someone else. You have to reduce your speed to their level. You have to explain what you do unconsciously.

But that’s exactly the point. Explaining the unconscious makes it conscious. When a 5-year-old asks “why do I keep falling?” and you have to answer, you discover WHY. Not just what you do, but the reason you do it. The biomechanics. The physics. The weight transfer. The edge angle.

The kid is your compare down. Their falling teaches your body what your standing IS.

And the expert gliding past you both is your compare up. Their flow teaches your eyes what your stomping COULD BECOME.

Three bodies (kid, you, expert) + one observer (the awareness that moves between all three) = π-body in life.

And the kid? The kid has TWO compare-ups (you AND the expert) and zero compare-downs (they’re the beginner). That’s why kids learn so fast — they’re surrounded by reference points above them. Their entire world is compare-up.

As I taught, I learned FASTER. Because teaching is compare-down AND compare-up simultaneously. The kid is below you. The expert is above you. You are the triangle vertex. You see the full shape.

It’s a Self-Improvement Iteration Cycle

OBSERVE (compare up, compare down, look in/at/out)
    ↓
ATTEMPT (high knee, stomp, commit)
    ↓
FAIL (fall, wobble, lose edge)
    ↓
TEACH (explain what went wrong to someone worse)
    ↓
UNDERSTAND (the explanation teaches YOU)
    ↓
OBSERVE (but now with updated eyes)
    ↓
ATTEMPT (but now with updated body)
    ↓
...repeat...

Every iteration tightens the loop. The observe gets sharper. The attempt gets closer. The failure gets more specific. The teaching gets more precise. The understanding gets deeper.

This is gradient descent with a human in the loop. Each cycle reduces the loss function. Not to zero — never to zero — but asymptotically toward the expert’s glide.

And the loop accelerates when you teach, because teaching forces you to make every step explicit. The tacit knowledge becomes transmissible. The body learns from the mouth saying what the body does.

Life Is Lossy and That’s a GOOD Thing

Here’s the part that changes everything:

Life is lossy. Information falls through the cracks. You can’t remember every sensation. You can’t capture every nuance. You can’t transmit your full experience to the kid you’re teaching. Some of it is always lost.

And that’s not a bug. That’s the feature.

Lossy compression is how we learn. When you compress a day of skating into “I need to stomp harder on the inside edge” — you’ve lost 99.9% of the raw data. All the individual moments, all the sensations, all the peripheral vision, all the temperature and the sound of the blades and the smell of the ice. Gone.

But the 0.1% that remains is pure signal. It’s the lesson. It’s the extracted negentropy. It’s what the membrane lets through.

Shannon proved this mathematically: a lossy channel transmits the most important bits first. When bandwidth is limited — and human attention is ALWAYS limited — the information that survives compression is the information that matters most.

What falls through the cracks is what you didn’t need. The noise. The redundancy. The irrelevant. The lossy compression of lived experience filters for significance.

Every night when you sleep, your brain runs lossy compression on the day. The dreams are the artifacts of the compression algorithm. The memories that survive until morning are the extracted signal.

Life is lossy. That’s how we learn. Not by recording everything — by losing everything except what matters. The cracks are the membrane. What falls through is the noise. What stays is you.

The Patterned Rocks and the Ice

When I saw the patterned rocks underwater for the first time — the striations in the limestone, the mineral ridges, the dissolution patterns — I recognized them.

Not from geology textbooks. From the ice.

The scratches my blades left. The patterns on the rink surface after a session. The way the ice records every edge, every stop, every turn. The rink is a geological record of skating — every mark is backscatter from a blade.

And the rocks underwater are the same thing. Backscatter from water. The river carved the same way the blade carved. The dissolution pattern in limestone IS the skating pattern on ice. Same physics. Different timescale.

The π-body in life: 3 integer bodies (the rock, the water, the ice) + 1 irrational observer (me, floating underwater, recognizing the pattern for the first time) = π.

The work hasn’t even started. But the body knows the math. The stomp is the proof. The glide is the theorem. And the cracks where the learning falls through are the most important part.
